ABSTRACT Seasonal changes in environmental conditions can influence the timing and distribution of infectious disease outbreaks. This field project analyses seasonal patterns in selected disease-outbreak records for Maharashtra using the EpiClim weekly district-level dataset, which is derived from outbreak information associated with India’s Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme (IDSP). The original EpiClim file contains 8,985 records covering 2009–2023 in its metadata; the uploaded analysis file contains records through 2022. For the core analysis, Maharashtra records from 2011–2019 were selected to provide a multi-year pre-COVID comparison period with substantially better reporting coverage than the earliest years. Three diseases were selected for detailed comparison: Dengue, Malaria and Acute Diarrhoeal Disease. The analysis examines reported outbreak records, reported cases, monthly distribution, annual variation and district coverage. The results show distinct seasonal profiles: dengue reports are concentrated mainly in August–October, malaria shows stronger concentrations around the monsoon and late-year months, while acute diarrhoeal disease has its largest reported case totals in May–July. These patterns indicate that different diseases do not share an identical seasonal cycle. The project also documents data-quality limitations, including missing death values, sparse reporting in some years, an anomalous non-numeric case entry and the fact that outbreak reports do not represent complete population incidence. Field observations and an interview are intended to provide local context for the quantitative findings. The study demonstrates how public health surveillance data can be cleaned, organised and analysed using data-science methods to identify seasonal patterns relevant to preparedness and preventive planning. Changes in rainfall, temperature, water availability, sanitation conditions, vector breeding conditions and human behaviour can influence when particular diseases are reported. Understanding these patterns is useful because public-health activities such as surveillance, awareness campaigns, vector control and preparedness can be planned before expected seasonal increases. India maintains disease surveillance through the Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme (IDSP), which is operated under the National Centre for Disease Control (NCDC). IDSP uses syndromic, presumptive and laboratory-confirmed reporting formats and also receives weekly outbreak reports from States and Union Territories. The programme is designed to monitor disease trends and detect and respond to outbreaks at an early stage. CHAPTER 2 – LITERATURE REVIEW Previous research has demonstrated that disease surveillance data can be used to investigate temporal and spatial patterns of infectious diseases. Studies of dengue and other epidemic-prone diseases commonly examine monthly or weekly case distributions and relate disease timing to environmental conditions. A 2021 study on monsoon-related diseases in Mumbai used secondary data from the Municipal Corporation of Greater Mumbai Epidemiological Cell to examine diseases including dengue, malaria, leptospirosis, acute gastroenteritis, typhoid, hepatitis and H1N1. The study provides a useful example of using municipal surveillance data to examine disease patterns across time. The EpiClim dataset used in the present project was created as a district-level weekly climate-health dataset for India. Its metadata describe epidemiological variables including disease, cases and deaths together with precipitation, leaf-area index and temperature. The dataset is intended for climate-health research and contains 8,985 records. [CITE ZENODO]. The NCDC description of IDSP explains that the programme uses S, P and L surveillance formats and separately receives weekly outbreak reports from States/UTs. This distinction is important for interpreting EpiClim: the records used here should be treated as reported outbreak observations rather than as a complete count of all infections in Maharashtra. 2.1 Research Gap A student-level analysis can add value by combining a structured district-level surveillance dataset with a simple, transparent seasonal comparison across multiple diseases. Rather than attempting complex forecasting, this project concentrates on identifying recurring monthly patterns, comparing disease profiles and documenting data limitations. 2.2 Summary of Literature Review The literature supports the use of surveillance data for temporal and spatial disease analysis, while also showing the importance of considering reporting systems, environmental variables and changes in healthcare or surveillance conditions when interpreting apparent trends. CHAPTER 4 – OBSERVATION AND ANALYSIS 4.1 Dataset Overview The uploaded EpiClim file contains 8,985 records. The Maharashtra subset contains 1,195 records covering 38 district labels and years 2009–2022. The core study period 2011–2019 contains 979 Maharashtra records. The selected diseases account for 771 records during the core period: 374 Acute Diarrhoeal Disease, 317 Dengue and 80 Malaria records. 4.2 Data Quality Observations The source file contains no exact duplicate rows. The Cases variable contains one non-numeric value ('3/1'), while the Maharashtra subset contains many missing Deaths values. Precipitation, LAI and Temperature also contain missing values in the Maharashtra subset. These observations demonstrate why data cleaning and cautious interpretation are necessary. 4.3 Dengue Seasonal Pattern During 2011–2019, the Maharashtra subset contains 317 Dengue outbreak records and 9,577 reported cases. The highest monthly reported case totals occur in September (1,947), October (1,772) and August (1,720). These three months together account for a substantial concentration of the reported Dengue cases in the study period. The number of Dengue outbreak records is also highest in September (57), October (50) and August (48). 4.4 Malaria Seasonal Pattern During 2011–2019, the Maharashtra subset contains 80 Malaria outbreak records and 2,963 reported cases. The largest monthly reported case totals occur in July (742), December (450) and June (429). The pattern therefore differs from Dengue: while July is prominent, a second concentration occurs toward the end of the year. 4.5 Acute Diarrhoeal Disease Seasonal Pattern During 2011–2019, the Maharashtra subset contains 374 Acute Diarrhoeal Disease outbreak records and 19,436 reported cases. The largest monthly totals occur in June (4,519), May (3,324) and July (2,893). The reported pattern is therefore concentrated earlier in the warm/rainy-season transition and differs clearly from the later Dengue peak. 4.6 Comparison of Diseases The comparison indicates that the selected diseases do not have an identical seasonal profile. Dengue is most concentrated in August–October, Malaria shows strong reported activity around June–July with another increase in December, and Acute Diarrhoeal Disease has its strongest reported case totals in May–July. This supports the usefulness of analysing diseases separately rather than treating all disease reports as one combined seasonal series. 4.7 Annual Variation Across all Maharashtra disease records in the uploaded dataset, the number of records varies by year. Within the 2011–2019 core period, Maharashtra has 83 records in 2011, 124 in 2012, 114 in 2013, 115 in 2014, 106 in 2015, 145 in 2016, 110 in 2017, 94 in 2018 and 88 in 2019. Such variation means that annual changes should not be interpreted simply as changes in underlying disease incidence. 4.8 District Coverage The Maharashtra data cover many districts, but coverage is not uniform across diseases or years. District-level summaries in the accompanying workbook should be used to identify where reported outbreak observations are concentrated. CHAPTER 5 – CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATION 5.1 Conclusion The analysis demonstrates that seasonal patterns in reported disease outbreaks can be identified using district-level surveillance data. In the Maharashtra records analysed for 2011–2019, Dengue, Malaria and Acute Diarrhoeal Disease show different monthly profiles. Dengue has its strongest reported concentration in August–October, Malaria shows a prominent July peak with additional late-year activity, and Acute Diarrhoeal Disease has its largest reported case totals in May–July. These differences suggest that seasonal preparedness should not be based on a single generic disease calendar. At the same time, the findings must be interpreted as patterns in reported outbreak records rather than estimates of all disease cases in Maharashtra. The EpiClim dataset is a secondary compilation derived from IDSP outbreak information, and reporting frequency, missing values and changes in surveillance conditions can influence the observed pattern. 5.2 Recommendations • Use disease-specific seasonal calendars for preparedness rather than assuming every disease peaks in the same month. • Strengthen awareness and preventive activities before the months in which reported outbreaks historically increase. • Use surveillance trends to support timely vector-control, sanitation and public-health communication activities. • Maintain consistent reporting and data-quality checks so that changes in reported counts can be interpreted more reliably. • Where possible, combine outbreak surveillance with routine case surveillance and local environmental information for a more complete assessment. • Repeat the analysis periodically as newer official data become available. 5.3 Limitations • The EpiClim dataset is a secondary compilation rather than the original IDSP database. • The records represent reported outbreaks and do not equal total disease incidence in the population. • Some Deaths, precipitation, LAI and temperature values are missing. • The uploaded CSV contains records through 2022 even though the Zenodo metadata describe the dataset as covering 2009–2023. • Reporting coverage varies by year, district and disease. • The 2020–2022 period may be affected by COVID-19-related changes in healthcare use and surveillance and is therefore not part of the core 2011–2019 seasonal comparison. • No causal relationship between climate variables and disease was established.
